# Kinstead

A little isometric village builder that runs in your browser — pixel-art rendering in the spirit of *Metropolis 1998* and the late-90s city sims, with a living village of tiny people going about their day.

**Play it:** https://daniuluo.github.io/kinstead/

## Features

- **Pixel-art isometric 3D** — the scene is rendered in real 3D (three.js), then drawn at low resolution with depth/normal outlines and crisp pixel upscaling, using a classic 2:1 isometric camera with a single fixed angle. Settings let you choose the pixel size or switch to a smooth, antialiased renderer with ambient occlusion.
- **Rotating view** — press `Q` / `E` or the rotate buttons beside `2D` to swing the camera a quarter turn around the ground at the middle of the screen and see the town from all four sides. The sun stays where it is in the sky, so shadows keep falling the same way across the ground and the far side of the town is the shaded side.
- **2D top-down map view** — press `T` or the `2D` button to swing the camera straight down for a grid-aligned map (it rotates too); zoom in and out with the wheel or the `+`/`−` buttons.
- **Procedural valley** — every seed grows a different landscape: rivers, lakes, hills, forests and rocky slopes, shown as a diorama. Pick a size: Small 32×32, Medium 48×48, Large 64×64 or Huge 80×80. Terraform by raising, lowering and levelling land, and plant trees.
- **Starter towns** — found a village in an empty valley, or take over one that's a few weeks along: a **riverside hamlet** (cottages, a woodcutter and a fishing hut by the river), a **market village** (a cobbled high street with a bakery, tavern, school and family houses), a **mill town** (farms, a workshop, a chapel and a water wheel powering the town) or a **crossroads village** (a coaching tavern, a smithy and a coach line out to every corner). Each card in the new-village window shows a little map of the town, its size, villagers and money, and picking one shows it behind the window.
- **Organic village** — no two buildings look the same. Each one is generated from a seed: walls, roofs (thatch, tile, slate, shingle), half-timbering, chimneys, dormers, shutters, flower boxes, gardens and fences. Paths are worn into the grass and meander a little.
- **Upgradable buildings** across homes, commerce (market, bakery, tavern), industry (farm, woodcutter, fishery, workshop, builder's yard), services (well, clinic, school, watch, chapel, town hall), and parks and decor. Most buildings have three or four levels, up to a Market Hall, Hospital, Academy or Minster. Upgrades need happiness and service coverage, and each option shows what changes (homes, jobs, earnings, reach, upkeep…) before you commit.
- **Branching homes** — a Cottage becomes a Family House, which then goes one of two ways: a **grand home** (Villa → Manor House → Grand Manor: few, wealthy residents who pay much more tax) or **flats** (Townhouse Flats → Tenement → Mansion Block: many households on one plot). Pick a **style** for any home or shop upgrade — timber frame, limewash, painted, brick, stone or clapboard — and tick **Preview in town** to see it in place before building. Each style builds differently, not just in different colours: timber frame has steep roofs, a jettied upper floor and a central stack; limewash has thatch or half-hipped roofs, a chimney up the outside wall and a deep front garden; painted is a symmetrical Georgian front with a hipped roof, a centred door and a chimney at each end; brick has gables, bay windows, round-topped windows and paired stacks; stone has low slate or saltbox roofs, deep-set round-topped windows and an outside stack; clapboard has a saltbox roof, dormers, shutters and a deep porch. On a bigger plot a home can become a **Country Mansion** or a block of **Apartments**, and on a 2×2 estate a **Manor Estate** or a **Mansion Quadrangle**.
- **Construction** — nothing appears instantly. Builders from the **Builder's Yard** walk to each site and work round the scaffolding; a building goes up in stages (pegged-out plot and materials, foundations, walls rising, roof) and only progresses while someone is working on it. Hired hands from the village start every site straight away and keep at it until the town's builders arrive, so a job never waits for the walk out from the yard; with no builders in town, they do all of it. Each job takes a third of the work it first did (`BUILD_PACE` in [`src/defs.js`](src/defs.js)): a cottage goes up in under two game hours, and bigger buildings still need the builders. Tick **Priority** on a site's panel to send the builders there before anywhere else; a flag marks it until it's finished. A sandbox switch builds instantly.
- **Research tree** — press `K` for 48 projects in 14 branches (building works, homes, stone, brick, colour, trade, leisure, industry, farming, water, power, public services, learning and transport). Each branch has levels: unlocking a building gives its first levels, and later projects unlock its higher upgrades and other paths. Capabilities are researched too: **Joining** neighbours, **Plot Surveying** (expanding and buying garden strips), **House Moving** and **Great Plots** (2×2 estates, and the buildings they open up). Click a project to see pictures of what it unlocks. Research points come from town halls, schools, colleges, chapels and your villagers (1 for every 40) each day, and later projects cost more, so a town works through the tree over many years. The top bar shows the current project's progress (or your banked points) and points per day, and the research window lists where each day's points come from.
- **Water and electricity** — a **Utilities** menu (`U`) with wells, water towers and river pumps (each draws water for a number of people), and power from water wheels, wind generators and coal power stations. Water flows through mains under the streets and power along them on poles and wires, to every road-connected building, so it doesn't matter where on the roads a well or generator stands; when demand outstrips supply, some buildings go dry or dark. Powered homes are happier, powered businesses earn more, and the grandest upgrades need electricity. With **Underground Cables** researched, the power lines can be buried, one street at a time from a road's panel or the whole town at once from a generator's: the poles and wires come down and the street looks the better for it.
- **What it takes to work** — buildings need more than a road. Street lamps are electric and go dark without power; homes without it fall back to dim candlelight. Powered machinery (manufactories, foundries, paper mills, timber yards, furniture works, waterworks and pumping stations, hospitals, university laboratories) stops or slows without power, and shops and inns past their first levels do little trade after dark without electric light. Bakeries, taverns and inns, paper mills, cider works, clinics and almshouses, fire stations and fountains need running water; homes without it can't upgrade, expand or grow their family. Services, schools and utilities only work as well as they are staffed: a clinic with nobody in it cares for nobody, and a school has as many places as its teachers can manage. Stopped works go dark, their chimneys cold and their wheels still; small badges over a building show everything it is missing, side by side (red for what stops it, amber for what holds it back), the building panel says what is missing and what to do, and the Issues panel groups them ("2 workshops stopped — no power"). Early buildings — cottages, wells, farms, woodcutters, fishing huts, builders — need none of this. The rules for each type are in `NEEDS` in [`src/defs.js`](src/defs.js).
- **Expanding plots** — two steps. First **take the land** beside or behind a building: an empty plot ($60 of land, plus clearing any trees), a strip of a cottage's garden, or a small neighbour bought out at what its building is worth and demolished (it asks twice, and shows the plot it would take). Taking land is instant and the building stays as it is, with the new land as its garden. Then, under **Upgrade**, choose what to build across the bigger plot: the bigger buildings it opens up (a Country Mansion or Apartments, a High School or a College), or **Extend** the building as it is to hold 50% more, for what that extra half is worth: a home, shop or service is built on to, with the house carrying on along the same front in the same walls, windows, eaves and roof (its ridge a little lower), or a back wing a storey lower behind it, making an L, or a U on a 2×2 estate, with no second door or chimney; works get a work shed and yard, utilities a tank or transformer house, parks more garden. With Great Plots researched, a double-plot building can take the two plots beside it for a 2×2 estate ($120 of land) and then be rebuilt as its estate counterpart, or extended. Land is only offered where the bigger plot opens up something bigger to build.
- **2×2 estates** — a double-plot building grown onto the two plots beside it can be rebuilt as its 2×2 counterpart, at the level it had reached, each with its own model over the whole plot: a Country Mansion becomes a **Manor Estate** (wings round a forecourt, a portico, formal gardens and a stable court; then the Ducal Seat, with an orangery), an Apartment Court a **Mansion Quadrangle** (four blocks of flats round a garden court), an Arcade a **Corn Exchange**, an iron-and-glass **Covered Market Hall** and a domed **Grand Galleria**, an Inn a **Coaching House** (a carriage arch through to a yard of stables and a coach house), a Joinery the **Timber Works** (a sawmill, drying sheds and a log yard; then Steam Sawmills with a tall stack), a Parish School a **Cathedral Close** (a cathedral with towers and, later, a spire, and its school round a green), an Almshouse an **Infirmary Court** (rooms and wards round a garden court behind a gatehouse), a High School or a College a **College Quad** (a gate tower, a chapel, a library and a great hall round a lawn) and a Town Park a **Landscape Park** (a lake, an avenue up to a temple, then a bandstand and a palm house). Each is a step up from its double-plot building grown to 2×2, priced in line with the rest (`node scripts/economy.js` sets them side by side), and the building guide and the research tree show them. Estates don't grow any further.
- **Demolition** — a crew pulls buildings down over time; the people inside move out first.
- **Restyling** — a home or shop can change style without being upgraded: its panel's **Restyle** section lists every style you have researched, and builders rebuild its fronts and roof in the one you pick, for 30% of what the building is worth (the same surcharge as changing style during an upgrade).
- **Joining and moving** — select a building next to another to **join** them on a double plot: two homes make a Terrace, a home and a shop a mixed-use **Shophouse** (shop below, flats above), two shops an Arcade, a tavern and a shop an Inn, a chapel and a school a Parish School, a clinic and a chapel or home an Almshouse, a well or a pump and a garden or statue a Fountain Garden, two workshops or woodcutters a Joinery, and gardens, flowers and statues a Park. A villa takes in a villa or cottage next door, or a green or flower bed as its grounds, as a **Country Mansion**, and a block of flats takes in flats or a cottage as an **Apartment Court** (the grand home keeps its look). A middle school and a school or another middle school make a **High School**. Three joins make buildings of their own: a tavern and a workshop a **Brewhouse** (a brewing tower with a louvred ventilator, a copper house and a taproom; then a brick **Brewery** with a works stack and a horse dray), two watch posts a **Watch House** (a station with a blue lamp, a watch tower and a walled yard of cells, keeping a wider area safe than the two posts; then a **Police Station** with a clock tower and a police wagon), and a fishing hut and a market stall a **Fish Market** (slabs of the catch under an open roof, an ice house, nets and a boat; then a **Fish Hall** with arched openings under a glazed roof, and a smokehouse). Five more make buildings of their own:
  - two herbalists make a **Cottage Hospital**: an entrance block with the red cross and a ward with a verandah, reaching much further. It becomes a three-storey **General Hospital** with wings and a horse ambulance.
  - two schoolhouses make a **Board School**: a tall brick range with girls' and boys' porches, a bell turret and a walled playground, with far more places. It becomes a three-storey **Large Board School**.
  - a chapel and the cottage next door make a **Chapel & Vicarage**: the chapel keeps its look, the minister's family lives beside it, and gravestones, a yew and a lych-gate fill the churchyard. It becomes a **Church & Rectory** with a clock and a spire.
  - a builder's yard and another or a woodcutter make a **Timber & Building Yard**: more builders, working faster with their own timber shed. It becomes **Building Contractors** with a treadwheel crane.
  - a cottage and a workshop make a **Craftsman's House**: the family over the workshop, with far less smoke. It becomes **Artisans' Row**, three houses with workshop windows and trade signs.

  A tavern, a watch post, a fishing hut, a herbalist, a schoolhouse, a chapel, a builder's yard or a workshop on a grown double plot can be rebuilt as these too. A joined building takes the higher of the two levels, but never one the town hasn't researched or grown into yet, and the join button warns when the new building has room for fewer people than live in the two. **Move** any building for a fee: builders take it down, a cart hauls the materials, and it goes back up on the new plot.
- **All paths** — every building's panel has a **Paths** button beside its type name (and the building guide an **All paths** link) that opens **Upgrades & joins**, a tech tree of everything it can become. Each row is a building with its levels as nodes, left to right, and a line leaves from the exact level a path opens at: another path after the top level, or a side path from one level. Rebuilds on a double plot or a 2×2 estate, the next stage of schooling and joins with a named neighbour, which any level can take, leave from the start of the row. Lines are coloured by kind and dashed while locked. Rows say how they are reached, the building's own upgrade paths sit right under it, where it stands now is highlighted, locked levels show what they wait for, and clicking a row or level opens its guide.
- **Hillsides** — on a slope a building is let into the hill rather than raised on a platform to the plot's highest corner: its floor sits up to a quarter of a storey lower, never below the ground at its front door, so the door stays at street level. Where the hill falls away the rest is a lower floor in dressed stone with a string course, small windows that light up at night and a door on the side most open to the slope, like a house built into the hillside.
- **Streets** — buildings come forward into the verge of the street in front of them, while facing buildings and street corners stay clear of each other. Little paths run from each front door to the street, and villagers step out of real front doors and walk down them.
- **Gardens and land** — cottages sit at the front of their plot with a real back garden (vegetables, washing lines, sheds, hens). Big homes can buy a strip of a neighbouring cottage's garden; their owners pay the cottage's owners, both plots change size together and nothing ever overlaps.
- **Busy places** — parks, gardens and squares hold only so many visitors at once (more for bigger and upgraded ones; the park's panel shows how full it is). When one is full, villagers try the nearest park with room, or stay home. A park needs a road to it before anyone can visit.
- **Roads** — dirt paths, cobblestone and paved streets drawn as crisp surfaces with edging stones, plus wooden bridges over water with sloped approaches. Street lamps light up at night once the town has electricity. Where a road turns a corner it sweeps round in a curve, and the inside corners of junctions are filled in with a matching curve, kerbs, door paths and power poles following along; villagers cut the corner along the same curve. (Roads onto a bridge stay square for the ramp.)
- **Whole stretches of road at once** — **Select roads** in the Roads menu (or **Select stretch** in a road's panel) picks the run of road between two junctions with a click, more runs with Shift-click, or every road in a dragged box or line (`Tab` switches). The panel shows what upgrading every tile one level, downgrading it or taking it all up costs or pays back (a quarter of the price comes back), what can't change and why (paved streets go no higher, the road out of the valley must stay), and who would be cut off: buildings left without a road or the way out of town, coach lines left without a route, buildings that step back from the street. Anything cut off takes a second click. Hovering an action shows on the map which tiles change and who loses out. `Backspace` steps back through your picks, right-click clears them; it works the same in the 2D map and at any rotation.
- **Dressing a street** — from the same panel, a picked stretch can become a **tree-lined avenue** (street trees on both verges), a **hedged lane** (low hedges along both sides), a **lamp-lit street** (a lamp every other tile, lit at night when the town has power) or, on paved streets, a **planted boulevard** (a planted median). Each has a price per tile and a little upkeep, and makes the homes and shops beside it a little nicer to live by. Trees and hedges leave gaps at junctions, bends, driveways, lamps and poles, and take the verges: buildings that stood forward into the street step back behind them (the panel says how many). Swapping or removing a dressing, or taking up its road, pays back a quarter of its price; downgrading a boulevard below paving takes its median off. The prices are in `DRESSINGS` in [`src/config.js`](src/config.js).
- **Transit** — horse-drawn **coach lines** loop between coach stops. **Railways** have track (with curves), stations and steam trains. Trains never reverse, so a rail line needs a loop of track through its stations; trains drive forward around it, ease in and out of stations, and stop centred on each platform (up to three trains per loop). Villagers on long trips walk to a stop, wait there, ride, and pay a fare.
- **Animals** — a few deer and foxes roam the woods, rabbits the meadows, and mallards paddle on the lakes and river. Sheep, cows and chickens graze on the farms. They wander slowly and unpredictably, check the ground a step ahead so they steer around buildings, rarely cross roads, keep to their habitat, and bolt from villagers who get too close. Animals are drawn life-size next to the villagers.
- **Villagers** — little abstract figures drawn to scale with the buildings (a grown-up comes up to about four fifths of a front door), walking at a slow stroll (half the pace they once had; the coach and cart horses walk at half pace too) on two stubby legs with a soft shadow at their feet. Every resident has a name (families share a surname, and names rarely repeat), an age, an education, a home, maybe a job, a wage, savings, living costs, a family, friends (workmates, neighbours, classmates) and one or two personality traits (early riser, night owl, homebody, sociable, big spender, thrifty, outdoorsy, devout, hard-working…) that shape their daily routine. Press `P` for the **Villagers** directory: search, filter and sort everyone in town, then click a name to follow them. A building with several households lists its residents family by family. Single-family homes take one family: a cottage, family house or villa holds one household (a Manor House, Grand Manor or Country Mansion two, a Stately Home three, the 2×2 estates four or five, a terrace one per house in the row), and newcomers to them come as one big family that all but fills the house, children, grown-up sons and daughters and grandparents among them. Since each newcomer household needs a home of its own, cottages (5) and family houses (00) are cheaper than they were and families take up empty homes sooner, so a town grows at about the pace it did when homes filled up with lodgers. The family make-up and arrival pace are `FAMILY_KIDS`, `FAMILY_KIDS_BIG`, `ARRIVE_RATE` and `ARRIVE_MAX` in [`src/config.js`](src/config.js). Flats, apartments and shophouses take any number of households. In a town from before this, a home with more households than it takes lets the newest move on to a home with room, a few a day, and now and then one moves away when the town has nowhere for it.
- **Ages and schooling** — a year passes every two days. Babies are born, children grow up, young adults move out of their parents' home, older villagers retire and eventually die. Children go to the school that fits their age (elementary, middle, high school) and some young adults go on to college or university. Each school has a fixed number of seats. Better-educated workers earn more, and skilled jobs need them. A school upgrades two ways: **larger** (more seats at the same stage) or the **next stage** (Middle School → High School → College → University), and the later stages need a bigger plot.
- **Happiness** — click the happiness figure in the top bar to see what feeds it across the town (water, health care, schooling, safety, culture, electricity, scenery, shops, transport, smoke, taxes, crowding, work and road access): how many points each adds now, how many the average villager could gain, how many homes miss out, and a button that takes you to the fix. A home's panel lists its own gaps first with what to do, and each villager's page shows their mood and what would make them happier.
- **Windows and issues** — drag any window by its title bar and keep several open at once; `Esc` closes the front one. The **Issues** panel in the lower left (`I`) lists the town's bottlenecks: homes out of reach of health care, schools or safety, school places short, power or water short, workplaces stopped or slowed for want of power, water or workers, sites waiting for builders, skilled jobs nobody can fill. Click an entry to jump to the buildings involved, one after another.
- **Economy** — money in dollars: taxes, shop visits, production, upkeep, a budget screen with charts, and RCI demand bars. New families arrive from the edge of the map when the village is attractive; unhappy households leave.
- **Time and seasons** — a day lasts 8 minutes at 1×; sunrise and sunset, lit windows, lamps, lantern-carrying villagers, fireflies, then spring blossom, autumn leaves and winter snow.
- **Weather** — most days are fair, but now and then clouds roll in for a few hours and the heaviest spells bring rain (snow in winter), with a soft hiss of rain. The weather follows the town's seed and clock, so everyone in a multiplayer town sees the same sky.
- **Soundtrack** — a gentle folk soundtrack made up as it plays: a lute picking out chords, a flute or bells on top and a warm pad underneath. It slows into a minor key at night and plays quieter in the rain. It is synthesised in the browser, so there are no music files. Turn it off with the note button in the top bar, or set its volume in Settings.
- **Building guide** — hover over a card in the build menu for a summary of what the building does (cost, jobs, homes, reach, visitors, upkeep, what it grows into). Pick one and the Building guide window shows every level with its needs, and the other paths it can take.
- **Crowds** — every place villagers visit holds only so many at once: shops, pubs and theatres, chapels, clinics (villagers now go to see the doctor, the old most often) and parks. A full place sends people on to the nearest one of the same kind with room, or they stay home. Hover over a building or open it to see its visitors.
- **Speed and sandbox** — pause, or run at 1×, 2×, 4×, 8×, 16× or 32× (`[` and `]` step through them). Four sandbox switches, available for a new game or from Settings: unlimited money, unlock every building, upgrade without meeting needs, and instant construction.
- **Pop-ups** — money pop-ups, notices, hover tips, the name tag over a followed villager and the warning badges over buildings with no power, water or workers can each be switched off in Settings, or all at once with the bell button or `N`.
- **Multiplayer** — host your town and send friends a short invite code; they join from their own browsers and build the same town with you, live. Everyone sees each other's cursors and what they are building. See [Multiplayer](#multiplayer).
- **Saves** — in this browser (autosaved every game day, every minute and a half, and when you switch away from or close the tab; when a different town takes the autosave, say when you start a new village, the last one is kept as **Previous town**), as plain `.json` files, or to **Google Drive**. **Download save (.json)** sits at the top of the Save window (or press `Ctrl+Shift+S`), every saved slot has its own download button, and you can drop a `.json` save onto the game to load it.
- **Merge villages** — join two saved towns into one valley, from the Save window or the title menu. While playing, the map-pin button in the top bar merges a village into your town: a saved one, or a brand-new one (an empty valley or a starter town). You drag it into place right in the game view. See [Merging villages](#merging-villages).

## Multiplayer

Build one town together, live, with up to eight players.

**To host:** open the town you want to share and press `M` (or the people button in the top bar), then **Host this town**. On the title screen, **Multiplayer…** offers *New village and host* or *Continue and host*. You get a six-character invite code such as `K7Q-M2P`. Send it to your friends, or use **Copy invite link** to send a link that opens the game with the code filled in. Keep your tab open: your computer runs the town for everyone.

**To join:** open the game, choose **Multiplayer…** on the title screen (or press `M` in a town of your own, which is autosaved first), type your name and the code, and press **Join**. You wait on a *Waiting for the host to let you in…* screen until the host says yes; then the host's town loads and from then on follows the host's live. Everything you do (building, roads, track, terrain and trees, demolishing, upgrades with a chosen style, expanding, joining and moving buildings, research, taxes, transit lines, the town's name and the game speed) is sent to the host and applied for everyone. The sandbox switches stay with the host.

**Who's in is up to the host.** Each newcomer asks first: the host sees *Mallory wants to join* under the top bar, with **Let in** and **Turn away**. The Multiplayer window lists the players, with buttons to make one a visitor (who can look but not build) or remove them, and has the switches for asking, locking the town and what newcomers join as, plus **New code**. See [Safety](#safety).

The players box in the top bar shows who is connected. Each player's cursor appears in the others' towns as a ring in their colour with their name and what they are holding, and whatever building they have selected is outlined. If a guest leaves, everyone else carries on. If the host stops hosting or closes the tab, the guests are told and can keep playing the town on their own, save it in their browser or download it.

### How it works

- **Host-authoritative.** Only the host runs the simulation: the clock and speed, money, construction, villagers' homes, jobs and schools, arrivals and departures, transit and research. Guests don't run their own copy of the economy, which would drift apart (the simulation uses `Math.random` in many places).
- **One command dispatcher.** Every player action, in single-player too, is a small command object such as `{ t: 'place', type: 'house', x: 10, z: 12 }`, handled by [`src/commands.js`](src/commands.js). Single-player and the host apply it at once. A guest sends it to the host, which checks it with exactly the same `World` rules it uses for its own actions. A command carries only what the player chose, never options that bend the rules, and malformed ones are refused.
- **Snapshot, then deltas.** A guest joins with a snapshot of the town: an ordinary save plus each building's entrance and each villager's school place. Five times a second the host compares the town with what it last sent and sends only what changed: buildings and building sites, villagers, roads and how their streets are dressed, track, land heights, trees per tile, transit lines, money, research and the clock. Every 15 seconds it sends every building and villager in full (a keyframe), so nothing can drift for long. A ready-grown town of 200 buildings and 650 villagers costs the host about 0.6 ms per update and under 20 KB/s at 32× speed ([`src/net/sync.js`](src/net/sync.js)).
- **Villagers on guests are simulated locally, for looks.** Who lives and works where, and who arrives, leaves, is born or dies, comes from the host. A guest's own copy of the routines then walks them about, puts builders to work on the scaffolding and runs the coaches and trains, so its streets look alive without streaming hundreds of positions. Individual villagers may therefore be in a different spot on each screen. The money from shop visits and fares is always the host's ([`src/net/replica.js`](src/net/replica.js)).
- **A tab in the background.** A browser gives a background tab no animation frames, so a host whose tab is hidden keeps the town running for its guests in larger, undrawn steps. Browsers may slow such a tab down further after a while, so a host should keep the game in view.

### Safety

Every other player is treated as untrusted.

- **The host lets people in.** *Ask me before anyone joins* is on by default. A newcomer waits while the host gets a prompt (*Mallory wants to join · Let in / Turn away*) and sees nothing of the town until let in. If nobody answers within two minutes they're told so; at most four people can wait at once, and someone turned away can't ask again for 30 seconds. A connection that has been turned away is ignored from then on, even if it says hello again.
- **Lock and new code.** **Lock the town** turns away every newcomer, and anyone still waiting, without disturbing the players already in. **New code** swaps the invite code for a fresh one: the old code stops working at once (its PeerJS id leaves the signalling server), and everyone already in stays connected.
- **Removing a player.** **Remove** in the players list disconnects them with *The host removed you from the town*; like guests of a host who leaves, they can keep a copy of the town. For the rest of the session the same browser tab (a guest keeps one PeerJS id per tab, reloads included) and the same name (ignoring case and spacing) are turned away automatically, and the host is told. Someone determined could still come back from a new tab under another name, which is why asking stays on by default; a new code shuts them out unless someone passes it on. **Allow back** undoes a removal.
- **Builders and visitors.** Builders can do everything a guest can. Visitors can look around and point with their cursor, but the host refuses every command they send, with the reason, and their own game says why straight away. The host can switch any player either way and choose what newcomers join as. A player with no role at all is treated as a visitor.
- **Guests can only ask.** The host checks every guest command with the same World rules as its own; commands are looked up by their own names only (no `__proto__` or `constructor` tricks), the sandbox switches stay with the host, and a guest can send at most 15 actions and 25 cursor moves a second.
- **Size limits.** A guest's messages may be 256 KB at most and the host's 64 MB; anything announced as bigger is dropped rather than buffered, and a connection that doesn't introduce itself within 20 seconds is closed.
- **No markup gets through.** Names of players, villagers, towns and transit lines that arrive from another player, a loaded save or a merged village are stripped of markup and control characters and capped in length ([`src/util/text.js`](src/util/text.js)), colours must be plain `#rrggbb` values, and the UI escapes everything it shows as well.
- **Joining is always a click.** An invite link only fills in the code; you choose whether to join.
- **Your address.** As in any peer-to-peer game, players you connect with can see your internet (IP) address. Only share codes with people you trust.
- `scripts/net-check.js` plays a hostile guest and a hostile host (prototype keys, markup in names, floods, oversized messages) and checks that each is refused or cleaned. It also covers letting in, turning away, nobody answering, the waiting limit, removing (and the same tab or name coming back), locking, a new code and visitors' commands.

### Why PeerJS

The site is static (GitHub Pages), so players need a public way to find each other. Kinstead uses [PeerJS](https://peerjs.com/) 1.5.5, loaded from jsDelivr with a pinned version and a Subresource Integrity checksum, the first time someone hosts or joins. It was picked over Trystero and similar options for three reasons:

- Its free cloud signalling server lets the host claim a chosen peer id, so the invite code simply *is* the peer id: `K7Q-M2P` is the peer `hearthvale-K7QM2P` (the prefix keeps the game's old name).
- It reports an unknown id explicitly, so a wrong or expired code gets a clear "no town is open with that code" message rather than an empty room.
- It configures STUN and PeerJS's public TURN relay by default, so players behind awkward home networks can usually still connect.

The signalling server only introduces the browsers. The town itself travels directly between them over a WebRTC data channel, or through the TURN relay when no direct route exists. Problems get a plain message: the library couldn't load, the PeerJS server can't be reached, no town has that code, the connection couldn't be opened (a strict firewall), the town is full or locked, or the host turned you away or removed you.

## How the buildings are made

Everything is generated in code — there are no model files, textures or sprites. [`src/render/geo.js`](src/render/geo.js) builds low-poly shapes (boxes, bevelled boxes, roof prisms, cylinders) and merges them into one mesh per building with per-vertex colours and a glow channel for lit windows. [`src/render/buildings.js`](src/render/buildings.js) has a recipe per building type and level, and every building's random seed picks its materials, colours, roof, windows and yard. Brick courses and roof tiles are drawn by a shader, and the pixel-art look comes from a low-resolution post-process pass.

## Merging villages

**Save → Merge villages…** (or **Merge villages…** on the title menu) joins two towns into a new one. While playing, the map-pin button in the top bar (**Merge a village into this town**) opens the same window with your town as town A. Neither save is changed.

1. **Pick town A and town B.** Each can be a save in this browser, the town you are playing, a `.json` save you upload or drop onto its column, or **a brand-new village**. A brand-new village is either an empty valley (pick its seed and size; it comes with its founding families, as in a new game) or one of the starter towns. Town A keeps its name, its place and its road out of the valley; town B joins it.
2. **Arrange them**, in the game view or on a map.
   - **Place it in the town** (when town A is the town you are playing) pauses the game and sets the incoming village down as a real diorama beside your town. Drag it with the mouse; the blue, orange and dashed outlines show your town, the village and the merged map. **R** turns it a quarter turn, and the panel's buttons set it beside any edge or centre it on your town. The panel also shows what will be settled. **Enter** merges, and **Esc** cancels and carries on with the game as it was. The camera still pans and turns as usual: right-drag, WASD or the arrow keys, Q / E, and T for the top-down view.
   - **Arrange on a map**: a 2D map shows both valleys. Use **Left / Right / Above / Below** to set B beside A (the map grows to fit), drag B anywhere to overlap it or leave a gap, and **Turn B 90°** in quarter turns. B snaps to A's edges and centre as you drag. The picture becomes the actual merged valley a moment after you stop, and the panel shows the merged map size and everything that will be settled. A merged map can be up to 96×96: two big valleys side by side lose some empty countryside from their outer edges, never buildings. An arrangement too wide for that is refused with a reason.
3. **Merge.** The merged town loads, and a report lists what was moved, refunded, changed or dropped. If you were playing a town, it is saved in this browser first. The merged town is autosaved straight away and can be downloaded as `.json`.

How the join is made:

- **Ground** — each town keeps the land around its own buildings and streets. Where the two maps overlap, the join runs halfway between them, and any part of the square map that neither town covers becomes new countryside with its own hills and woods. In a band a few tiles wide each side of every join (auto-sized, or set **Blend width**), the heights are re-solved so each side keeps its own slopes and bumps while the difference in level is spread smoothly across the band (a Poisson blend). Buildings in the band rise or sink as rigid blocks, so their ground keeps its shape. A last pass eases any step steeper than the steepest in either valley. Ground colour (grass, sand, rock, shore) follows the blended heights. **Rivers and lakes** that reach a join are carried across: a river meeting water on the other side within a few tiles is joined to it by a curving channel; otherwise it runs on through new countryside to the map edge, or ends in a pool before the other town. Carried-on rivers keep clear of streets, and a street flooded by the blend gets a low embankment.
- **Buildings** — where two plots overlap, the more valuable one stays. The other moves to the nearest free plot with a road (turning a double plot if needed), or is refunded at full value if none is near. Buildings left in water, on ground too steep, beside water no longer (fishery, pump, water wheel), off their railway track, or cut off from every road are handled the same way. There is one town hall: the second becomes a **Plaza** of the same level, and the difference in value is refunded. Half-finished demolitions and buildings being moved away are dropped. Land deals survive only between two plots that both stayed put.
- **Roads** — a building beats a road, so streets under the other town's buildings are removed. Every separate piece of road is then joined to the network that leads out of the valley along the cheapest buildable route (bridges cost more than land). Stray scraps with nothing on them are cleared, a piece boxed in by buildings is removed, and a building left with no street gets a short lane, or moves. If neither town's road out of the valley still reaches the map edge, a new one is laid.
- **Ids and people** — town B's buildings, villagers and transit lines get new ids, and every reference moves with them: homes, jobs, families, transit stops, land deals and building moves. Villagers whose home vanished move into homes with room, households together where possible. If there is no room at all, they move away. Villagers whose workplace vanished look for new work. Nobody shares a whole name: the usual rule for newcomers picks a fresh first name.
- **Transit** — stops that are gone are dropped from their lines, and a line left with fewer than two stops closes, refunding its vehicles. Clashing line names and colours are changed.
- **Town** — research is everything either town discovered. Money is the two treasuries added together, plus refunds. The day and season are the later town's. Daily history is added up day by day, and sandbox switches carry over if either town had them on.

## Running locally

There is no build step: it's plain ES modules plus three.js from a CDN. It only needs to be served over HTTP, because modules don't load from `file://`.

On Windows, use the bundled PowerShell server (no Node or Python needed):

```powershell
powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File serve.ps1
```

Then open http://localhost:8080. Any static server also works, for example `npx serve .` or `python -m http.server 8080`.

## Google Drive saves

Cloud saves use your own Google account through Google Identity Services and the Drive REST API. They use the narrow `drive.file` scope, so the game can only see files it created itself. Those files live in a **Kinstead Saves** folder in your Drive (a **Hearthvale Saves** folder from before the game was renamed is still found and used).

You need your own OAuth Client ID (it's free):

1. In [Google Cloud Console](https://console.cloud.google.com/), create a project.
2. Go to **APIs & Services → Library**, find **Google Drive API** and enable it.
3. Go to **APIs & Services → OAuth consent screen**, choose **External**, and fill in the app name and your email. Add the scope `.../auth/drive.file`. While the app is in *Testing*, add your Google account under **Test users**.
4. Go to **APIs & Services → Credentials → Create credentials → OAuth client ID** and pick **Web application**. Under **Authorized JavaScript origins**, add:
   - `https://daniuluo.github.io`
   - `http://localhost:8080` (for local play)
5. Copy the Client ID (`…apps.googleusercontent.com`), then do one of:
   - paste it into `GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID` in [`src/config.js`](src/config.js) and push, so it works for everyone, or
   - paste it in-game under **Save → Google Drive**, or in **Settings**. This stores it in your browser only.

A Client ID isn't a secret, so committing it is fine.

### Smoke test

```bash
npm install three@0.170.0 --no-save
node scripts/check.js
```

This builds every building model at every level, joins every pairing in every join recipe (and checks a joined building never skips research, a villa leads when it takes in a cottage, and the join says who must move out), grows a double plot of each kind into a 2×2 estate and rebuilds it as its estate building, generates 25 valleys, simulates a town for six in-game days (walking villagers, a coach line, a train looping forward, a dead-end rail line refused), checks that animals never enter buildings or leave their habitat, checks the working rules (an unpowered manufactory makes nothing, street lamps go dark without power, a bakery and a clinic do less without water, a school without teachers has no places, and the Issues panel lists them), checks road shapes (bends, junction fillets, villagers keeping to the curve), the road selection (stretches between junctions, cost and refund totals, tiles that can't change, who would be cut off) and street dressings (prices, gaps at junctions, bends and driveways, appeal, upkeep, saving, removal with the road), and round-trips a save.

```bash
node scripts/net-check.js
```

The multiplayer test runs a host and two guests in one process, joined by in-memory links. It checks the command dispatcher (malformed or rule-bending commands are refused, the sandbox switches are host-only), has a guest build, lay roads, research, set taxes and speed, upgrade in a style, join, expand, move, terraform, plant and demolish, dress a street with hedges and upgrade that stretch of road, and open a coach line. It runs three days at 8× and checks that the towns stay identical throughout, that a keyframe repairs anything that drifted, that a second guest can join mid-game, that cursors are relayed, and that guests are told when the host closes the town. `npm run check` runs both tests.

### Playtest

```bash
node scripts/playtest.js 50 48 2024 economy
```

A bot plays a fresh town for a number of days (arguments: days, map size, seed, mode), building, laying roads, researching, upgrading, joining, expanding, moving and demolishing. Modes: `economy` (real money), `stress` (unlimited money, everything unlocked, lots of building every day), `chaos` (the same with instant construction, so far more upgrades, joins and moves) and `scale` (a big ready-grown town on an 80×80 map, to measure speed). Every day it audits the town (no overlapping buildings, no broken references, capacities respected, building sites always progressing, no builders idle while sites wait) and it prints anything wrong at the end, along with the day's budget, how many buildings are stopped or slowed and why, and how many double plots grew into 2×2 estates and were rebuilt as estate buildings (the busy modes grow an estate whenever one can). The bot researches water and power once the town grows, saves up for generators, puts wells by trades that need water, and builds homes rather than more of a workplace that already stands empty.

### Starter towns

```bash
node scripts/starters.js
```

Each starter town in [`src/world/starters.js`](src/world/starters.js) is a fixed map seed plus a short build script (streets, buildings, upgrades with styles, cobbles, a coach line, the research its founders bring and what it researches next). The town is **grown when you pick it**, in about 50 ms: every step goes through the normal world rules (it costs money and waits for the villagers and research it needs; buildings go up with instant construction), and between steps the real simulation runs a day at a time, so families move in, find jobs and school places, age, have children, pay taxes and research. A town a few weeks along has research behind it that a few weeks of play would not earn, so the points for its founders' projects are banked on the first day, and each of those projects is done as soon as it can be begun. The town's own research then carries on with the next projects on its list. The result is saved and loaded back through the normal save format, so a new game starts from exactly what a save of that town would load. The sim's dice are seeded, so a town comes out the same every time.

Growing the towns at runtime was chosen over shipping saved `.json` towns because a saved town is a snapshot of the rules at the time it was made: when buildings, capacities or the save format change, it goes stale and has to be regenerated by hand, while a grown town always follows the current rules. The test checks that this still works: it grows each town twice and compares them, fails if any script step could no longer be carried out, checks that research covers everything built and that homes are within capacity, then plays each town on for a few days with the full simulation, running the playtest audits every day, and round-trips a save. Add `--map` to print each town day by day and as a map, which helps when changing a build script.
### Merge test

```bash
node scripts/merge-test.js
```

This merges grown towns in every kind of arrangement: beside on each side, turned 90°, 180° and 270°, overlapping at several offsets, right on top of each other, apart with a gap, a small valley inside a big one, two big ones trimmed to fit, a town with itself, a brand-new village beside one and a starter town overlapping one, and two valleys whose rivers meet at the join. After each merge it checks the town invariants the playtest uses (no overlapping plots, no broken references, capacities). It also checks that every id is unique, there is one town hall, there is one road network that reaches the map edge and every building, nothing stands in water or on a cliff, no step on the map is steeper than in either valley, streets weren't flooded, everyone has a home, and buildings and villagers add up with the report. Research, money and the calendar must be combined correctly, and the save must round-trip. Several merges then run the simulation for a day. Add `--write <dir>` to save the test towns and a merged one as `.json` files to try in the browser.
